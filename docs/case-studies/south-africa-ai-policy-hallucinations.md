# South Africa National AI Policy Withdrawn Over Hallucinated Citations - April 2026

## Incident Overview

**Organization**: South African Department of Communications and Digital Technologies<br>
**Document**: Draft National Artificial Intelligence Policy<br>
**Date**: Published in the Government Gazette April 10, 2026; withdrawn April 27, 2026<br>
**Failure Mode**: [Tool Hallucination](../failure-modes/tool-hallucination.md)<br>
**Impact**: First known case of a national government withdrawing an official policy document because of AI-fabricated citations; at least 6 of 67 academic citations were invented<br>
**Technology**: Generative AI tool (suspected; not officially confirmed) used in drafting

## What Happened

South Africa's draft National AI Policy — a document meant to set the country's framework for governing artificial intelligence, including a formal AI ethics board — was published for public comment in the Government Gazette on April 10, 2026. Within roughly two weeks, the news outlet News24 reported that the policy's bibliography cited academic articles that do not exist. At least six of the document's 67 references were fabricated: real journals were named, but the specific articles, and in some cases the authors, were invented.

Editors at the *South African Journal of Philosophy*, *AI & Society*, and the *Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy* independently confirmed to News24 that the cited articles had never appeared in their pages. Some real researchers were credited with foundational work on topics they had never written about.

On April 27, 2026, Communications and Digital Technologies Minister Solly Malatsi withdrew the draft policy.

### The Minister's Response

Malatsi did not treat the fabrications as a minor formatting error. He called them an "unacceptable lapse" that "compromised the integrity and credibility of the draft policy," stated that "this failure is not a mere technical issue," and promised consequence management for those responsible for drafting and quality assurance. The most plausible explanation he offered: drafters used a generative AI tool and published the output without verifying a single reference.

The draft had been approved by Cabinet (on March 25 and April 1) and published for a public-comment period that was scheduled to run until June 10, 2026, before it was pulled. As of the withdrawal, no timeline was given for a corrected version, leaving the country without a national AI governance framework.

## Why This Case Matters

### The First Government to Pull a Policy Over AI Hallucinations

This is, as far as has been reported, the first time a national government has withdrawn an official policy because of AI-fabricated content. It moves the now-familiar "hallucinated citations" failure out of individual lawyers' filings ([Sullivan & Cromwell](sullivan-cromwell-bankruptcy-hallucinations.md)) and academic submissions ([ICLR 2026](iclr-2026-hallucinated-citations.md)) and into the machinery of state. A policy is authoritative by definition: other documents, regulations, and decisions build on it. Fabricated foundations in a governance document carry a contamination risk beyond any single filing.

### The Irony

The document undermined by unverified AI output was itself a policy meant to govern responsible AI use. The failure is a direct demonstration of the gap the policy was supposed to close: AI tools produce fluent, authoritative-looking text, and institutions deploy that text without verification.

## Technical Analysis

### LLMs Generate Citations, They Do Not Look Them Up

A language model produces a citation by predicting plausible-looking text — author, journal, year, title — not by querying a bibliographic database. A reference like a paper in *AI & Society* on AI governance is a probabilistic completion that matches the shape of a real citation without corresponding to any real article. Without an external verification step that checks each reference against the actual journal or a citation index, fabrications are statistically expected at scale.

### Why a 67-Reference Bibliography Is a Dangerous Surface

- **Plausible formatting**: The fake citations named real, topically appropriate journals and used standard academic citation structure, so they read as legitimate.
- **Volume**: A reviewer would need to check all 67 references against their sources to catch six fabrications — work that was evidently not done.
- **Authority bias**: A Cabinet-approved, Gazette-published government document carries an implicit assumption of accuracy that lowers scrutiny.

## Root Cause Analysis

### AI-Specific Failures

1. **No external verification step**: Citations generated by an AI tool were published without being checked against the named journals or any citation database.
2. **Confidence without grounding**: The fabricated references were indistinguishable in tone and format from the real ones, providing no signal that any particular citation was invented.

### Process Failures

1. **No quality-assurance gate that caught it**: The document passed Cabinet approval and Gazette publication with fabricated sources intact. The minister's own statement acknowledged a quality-assurance failure.
2. **Undisclosed and unverified AI use**: AI appears to have been used in drafting without disclosure and without the verification step that such use demands.
3. **Authority outran review**: The document's path to publication assumed correctness rather than verifying it.

## Government Response

- Minister Malatsi withdrew the draft policy on April 27, 2026, the same day the fabrications were widely reported.
- He publicly acknowledged the lapse and committed to consequence management for the drafting and quality-assurance failures.
- Civil-society and opposition figures called the episode "embarrassing and damning," and it became a continent-wide cautionary example of unsupervised AI use in government.
- No timeline was announced for a corrected draft, leaving South Africa's AI governance framework in limbo.

## Lessons Learned

### For Governments and Public Institutions

1. **Citations in authoritative documents must be verification-grounded**: Any reference in a policy, regulation, or official report should be checked against its actual source before publication. This is non-negotiable for documents others will build on.
2. **AI use must be disclosed and gated**: If AI is used in drafting, that use should be disclosed and paired with a mandatory verification step that cannot be skipped on the way to publication.
3. **Approval is not verification**: Cabinet sign-off and official publication confer authority, not accuracy. The review process must include someone actually checking the sources.

### For Anyone Using AI to Draft Reference-Heavy Documents

1. **Treat every generated citation as unverified**: A fluent citation is a prediction, not a lookup. Check each one against the real publication.
2. **The more authoritative the document, the higher the verification bar**: Fabrications in a national policy propagate further and are harder to retract than fabrications in a draft email.
